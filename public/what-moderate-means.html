What "Moderate" actually means, in µg/m³

It means the fine particulate is somewhere between 9.1 and 35.4 micrograms per cubic meter. The top of that band is nearly four times the bottom, and your phone shows you the same word at both ends of it.

Drew Hoover · 7 August 2026

The word is a bucket, and the bucket is enormous

The US AQI runs 0–500 and is cut into six named categories. "Moderate" is 51–100. For PM2.5 under the 2024 revision of the breakpoint table, that maps back onto concentration like this:

PM2.5, 24-hour basis, US EPA breakpoints (2024 revision), interpolated linearly within the category.

Six rows, one word. The last row is 3.9 times the first. If someone tells you the air is "Moderate", they have told you almost nothing about how much of it there is.

This is not a flaw so much as a consequence. The AQI exists to be broadcast on the evening news, and a scale that fits on a color swatch has to be coarse. But it means the sentence "it's only Moderate" is not the reassurance it sounds like, and the AQI 70 that ended a walk of mine — the day this whole project came out of — was around 19 µg/m³, comfortably mid-bucket.

"Moderate" is AQI 51; "sensitive groups" is Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups, AQI 101; "Unhealthy" is AQI 151. Every number is the lower bound — the exposure at which the category starts.

Two things fall out of that table the moment it is in one unit. Ozone's Moderate threshold (108 µg/m³) is above the World Health Organization's 2021 8-hour guideline of 100 µg/m³: air the EPA has not yet called Moderate is already over the WHO's number. And NO₂'s Unhealthy threshold is 679 µg/m³, against a WHO 1-hour guideline of 200. These are not contradictions — a regulatory index and a health guideline are different instruments with different jobs — but if you assumed the categories were health thresholds, they are not.

What this app does with them instead

Breathing Index does not show an AQI. It has a four-level scale defined by what the air makes you do:

  • 1 Easy — the air isn't a factor. Do anything.
  • 2 Noticeable — you'll feel it, but you can carry on as planned.
  • 3 Limiting — change the plan: shorter, slower, later, or elsewhere.
  • 4 Dangerous — outside is unsafe for you. Stay in filtered air.

Behavioral definitions, because they make a diary entry self-validating: you are reporting what you actually had to do, not scoring a vibe. And four levels with no midpoint force a lean.

On day one, before there is any diary to learn from, the app has to start somewhere. It starts with these — one ceiling per variable per level, meaning "at this exposure a sensitive person is potentially at this level":

Four honesty notes about that table, since it is the app's own guts:

  • The gas rows are not EPA. For ozone, NO₂, SO₂ and CO the app uses WHO (and one EU) guideline values, which are stricter than the EPA breakpoints — a sensitive-user ceiling should be. The one exception is CO level 4, which is hand-set and sits just above EPA's Unhealthy threshold.
  • Two of the numbers are mine, not anyone's. NO₂ level 3 and CO level 4 are hand-set steps between published values. They are labeled that way in the source, and they should be the first ones a real diary overrules.
  • The windows do not line up. The published breakpoints are averages — 24 hours, 8 hours, 1 hour — and the app compares them against the current hour and an 8-hour running maximum. One bad hour can therefore cross a 24-hour threshold that a real 24-hour mean would not. That bias warns early, which is the right direction for a ceiling, but it means these are not AQI categories and nothing in the app calls them that.
  • They are ceilings, and they are temporary. A prior can only ever raise a prediction, never lower one, and your own diary supersedes it. The whole point is to stop needing this table.

The part the table cannot do

Every number above is a population number. Not one of them was derived from anybody's actual lungs — they are thresholds chosen so that a large group of people, on average, is mostly fine, with a sentence appended about sensitive individuals. If you are the sensitive individual, the sentence is where you live and the number is somebody else's.

The replacement is embarrassingly simple and it is the reason this app exists. Rate your breathing, 1 to 4, when you think of it. The rating gets stored with every reading for that hour. After a few weeks you have your own breakpoints — real thresholds, in µg/m³, for the specific things that get you — and they will not look like the table above. Mine already don't.

Log the good days too. A 1 is a receipt that everything in that air, at those levels, was fine for you — it clears every variable at once, which is more than any bad day can do.
