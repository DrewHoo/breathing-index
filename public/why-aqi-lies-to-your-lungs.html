Why the AQI lies to your lungs

It doesn't lie, exactly. It answers a question honestly, and the question isn't yours. The AQI is the worst of several sub-indices, rounded into a category that was drawn for a population. You are not a population. You have one set of airways with specific opinions.

Drew Hoover · 7 August 2026

The two days that broke the number for me

I have asthma, and for years I have tried to make the published number and my own chest agree. In August 2026 they came apart badly enough that I started building software about it.

Amsterdam, 3–4 August 2026

The Dutch index (LKI) read 7–8 — the band they label onvoldoende, insufficient. It sounds like a warning. I walked around the city for hours. My lungs were working harder than usual and I noticed it, but nothing about the day changed because of them.

Hamden, Connecticut, 6 August 2026

The US AQI read 70 — Moderate. It sounds like nothing. I cut a walk short. That is the whole receipt: same lungs, milder-sounding label, worse day.

The number that sounded alarming was fine to breathe. The number that sounded fine was not. Twice is a coincidence; the mechanism underneath is not.

So I pulled the actual measurements for both days from Open-Meteo and AirNow rather than arguing with a label. The write-up is in the repo as docs/m1-findings.md; the short version is that the two days were not the same kind of bad air at all, and neither country's headline number said so.

  • Amsterdam was an ozone day. Instantaneous ozone peaked around 156 µg/m³ on the Monday afternoon. Fine particulate sat at 10–12 µg/m³ through the daytime hours. One elevated thing.
  • Hamden was two things at once. Fine particulate held at 13–14 µg/m³ all day with a smoke-like fine fraction, while ozone climbed underneath the headline all afternoon toward the Unhealthy-for-Sensitive-Groups band. Two elevated things, in the same air, at the same time.

One trigger versus two is a coherent explanation for "labored but functional" there and "can't finish the walk" here. It is also exactly the distinction a single number is built to destroy.

What the number actually is

The US AQI is not a measurement. It is a transformation, and it does three things to the air on the way to your screen.

1. It takes the max, then throws the rest away

Each pollutant gets its own sub-index on a 0–500 scale, computed from its own concentration breakpoints (40 CFR part 58, appendix G). The reported AQI is the highest of them, and the pollutant that produced it is named the "primary pollutant". Everything else that was in the air is not reduced, or averaged in, or flagged — it is simply not in the output.

On my Hamden afternoon, particulate won the max. The ozone ramping underneath it was invisible in the composite by construction. If you are one of the people for whom the combination is the problem, the index has silently answered a different question: how bad is the single worst thing? — not what is in this air?

2. It reports an average of a window you are not standing in

The breakpoints are defined over averaging windows: 24 hours for particulate, 8 hours for ozone and carbon monoxide, an hour for nitrogen dioxide and sulfur dioxide. Reporting smooths further still (US PM2.5 is published through NowCast, a weighted recent average).

An 8-hour ozone mean cannot spike when ozone spikes. It has to drag seven other hours along with it. On the Hamden day the 8-hour ozone sub-index went 50 → 72 → 113 → 133 across the afternoon and evening while the headline stayed comfortable for hours — the number was still describing lunchtime while I was outside at six.

3. It applies a label drawn for everybody

"Moderate" covers AQI 51–100. For PM2.5 under the 2024 table that is a concentration band from 9.1 to 35.4 µg/m³ — the top of the band is nearly four times the bottom, and both are the same word on your phone. AQI 70, the number that ended my walk, works out to roughly 19 µg/m³ of PM2.5.

The categories carry a cautionary line about unusually sensitive people, and that line is doing enormous work. It is where every asthmatic in the country has been filed. There is no version of "Moderate" that can be true for a marathon runner and true for me on a smoke day, so it is drawn for the middle and we are a footnote.

None of this is a scandal. It is a public-health instrument working as designed. The bug is thinking it was ever about you.

What to look at instead

Two things, and the second is the one that actually works.

The constituents, separately

Every source that publishes an AQI has the per-pollutant numbers behind it. Look at those. "PM2.5 at 19, ozone at 100 and climbing" is a completely different afternoon from "PM2.5 at 19, ozone at 40", and the composite renders both as 70. Once you are reading constituents, the useful questions get askable: is the particulate fine-mode, which points at smoke, or coarse, which points at dust and pollen? Is the ozone flat or is it on the afternoon ramp? Should I go now or at seven?

On the Hamden day that last question had a decisive answer sitting in the hourly data. Ozone was at a sub-index of 27–37 before ten in the morning and 113–159 from four until ten at night. Particulate was flat all day. Walking in the morning was free, and no headline number told me so.

Your own diary

Constituents tell you what is in the air. They do not tell you which of it gets you, and no published index can, because nobody has ever measured your lungs. That part only comes from you: a rating of your breathing, logged against the full set of readings for that hour, over and over until the pattern is not deniable.

This is more useful than it sounds, and the surprising part is that good days carry the most evidence. A day you breathed easily is a receipt that everything in that air was tolerable for you at those levels — every pollutant, cleared at once. A bad day with five elevated variables is ambiguous, and should be recorded as ambiguous rather than blamed on whichever one happened to be highest. That is the mistake the composite makes, and copying it into a personal tool would be a waste of a diary.

It is also the only method available to a person without a lab. Enough entries and you stop needing the label: you know your own thresholds, in real units, for the specific things that matter to you. Mine appear to be about smoke. Yours may be ozone, or humidity, or the combination on a hot day. The AQI will keep on not knowing.
