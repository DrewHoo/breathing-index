Smoke vs ozone: same AQI, different lungs

Two afternoons, three days apart, with ozone peaks within about eight percent of each other. One of them I walked through. The other one ended my walk. The difference was in the particulate, and there is a cheap way to see it that no headline number reports.

Drew Hoover · 7 August 2026

The two afternoons

These are model and station numbers I pulled for both locations on 6 August 2026, written up in docs/m1-findings.md. Nothing here is remembered; it is all in the spike output.

Amsterdam, 3–4 August — one elevated thing

  • Ozone peaked at 156 µg/m³ (Monday, 17:00).
  • PM2.5 sat at 10–12 µg/m³ through daytime hours.
  • Official label: Dutch LKI 7–8, onvoldoende.
  • Felt like: lungs working harder. Plans unchanged.

Hamden, Connecticut, 6 August — two elevated things

  • Ozone climbed 59 → 168 µg/m³ across the afternoon, with the 8-hour sub-index going 50 → 72 → 113 → 133 by 21:00.
  • PM2.5 held flat at 13–14 µg/m³ all day. Measured at the New Haven stations: PM2.5 AQI 55, the day's primary pollutant.
  • Official label at the moment I checked: US AQI 70, Moderate. Also, separately, an official Ozone Action Day.
  • Felt like: cut the walk short.

Comparable ozone, wildly different days. The variable that changed was the one the composite number had already discarded.

The fine-fraction fingerprint

Particulate matter is reported in two sizes, and the ratio between them is the most informative free number in air quality data.

  • PM10 is everything under 10 micrometers. Dust, road grit, crushed material, bits of pollen and mold — mechanical stuff, ground up and lofted. It is coarse-dominated.
  • PM2.5 is the fraction under 2.5 micrometers, and it is a subset of PM10, not a separate thing. Combustion makes it: fires, engines, and the secondary aerosol that forms in the atmosphere out of gases.

So divide one by the other. If PM2.5/PM10 is low — say 0.3 — most of the mass is coarse, and you are in dust, or agriculture, or a windy dry spell. If it is high, nearly all the mass is fine-mode, and something burned.

Hamden, 6 August, ~15:00 EDT

Model: PM2.5 14.0, PM10 15.0 µg/m³. Fine fraction 0.93.

Measured, same hour: PM2.5 sub-index 55, PM10 sub-index 18. Two instruments, same story.

Ninety-three percent of the particulate mass was fine. There is essentially no dust in that air. It is smoke, or something else that burned and drifted.

That single ratio is the difference between "there's grit in the air today" and "there is a fire upwind of me", and it takes one division on numbers both major data sources already hand out for free. The composite AQI does not report it, because the composite AQI reports the maximum sub-index and the name of the pollutant that produced it, and nothing else about what is in the air.

Why the two do different things to asthmatic airways

Careful here: I am an asthmatic with a data spike, not a pulmonologist, and this app is deliberately built to reason from your diary rather than from mechanism. But the general physiology is well documented — the EPA's Integrated Science Assessments for ozone and for particulate matter are the umbrella reviews — and it explains why "the same AQI" is a meaningless unit of comparison between them.

Ozone is a gas, and your effort is the dose

Ozone is a strong oxidant. It is only sparingly soluble, so it does not get scrubbed out in the nose and upper airway the way more soluble gases do — it travels down and reacts where it lands. The consequence that matters practically is that the harder you breathe, the more you get. Same concentration, twice the ventilation, roughly twice the dose. An ozone afternoon punishes exercise specifically, which is why the advice is always about moving your run rather than about staying in.

It also has a shape. Ozone is photochemical — it is built by sunlight out of precursor pollution — so it ramps through the day and collapses overnight, every time. On the Hamden day the 8-hour sub-index was 27–37 before ten in the morning and 113–159 from four in the afternoon until ten at night. That is not a bad day. That is a bad half-day with a free half-day attached, and knowing which half you are in is worth more than any label.

Smoke is particles, and they do not keep hours

Fine particulate is small enough to reach the deep lung rather than deposit in the upper airways, and wildfire smoke in particular is not inert dust: it carries an oxidative, chemically active organic load along with the carbon. For a lot of asthmatics — me, on this evidence — it produces the tight, unproductive, hours-later kind of bad rather than the sharp catch-your-breath kind.

And it has no diurnal escape hatch. A plume sits where the weather puts it. On the Hamden day PM2.5 was flat from morning to night: there was no hour of that day when the smoke was not there. That is why the timing advice on a smoke day is different from the timing advice on an ozone day, and why a tool that tells you "AQI 70" on both is not a tool.

Together, they are not an average

The Hamden afternoon was both at once — moderate smoke-like particulate the whole time, and ozone climbing into Unhealthy-for-Sensitive-Groups territory underneath it. Asthma research generally treats simultaneous oxidant gas and fine particulate exposure as compounding rather than as one substituting for the other, and a person standing outside at six in the evening got the whole combination.

A composite index cannot express "two things at once". max() has exactly one output, and it had already spent it on the particulate.

This is the finding that changed the design of my app. The first version scored days with a weighted maximum over sub-indices, which is a defensible thing to build right up until you meet a day like this one, where any composite either hides a trigger or invents an attribution. The engine now keeps ambiguity as ambiguity — several elevated variables produce a candidate set, and later diary entries confirm or exonerate the members — rather than picking a winner it has not earned.

How to tell which kind of day you are in

None of this needs a subscription. It needs three numbers you can already get.

  1. Fine fraction. PM2.5 divided by PM10. High means smoke or combustion aerosol; low means dust, and possibly a pollen day wearing a particulate costume.
  2. The ozone curve, not the ozone number. Look at the hourly forecast, find the ramp, and pick your hour. If your only free hour is in the ramp, that is useful to know in advance too.
  3. Whether more than one thing is elevated. Two moderate things is not a moderate day. If the composite is the only number you have, you cannot see this at all.

And then the part no dataset can do: write down how you actually felt, with the readings attached. Three smoke days that went badly and three ozone days that went fine is worth more, for you specifically, than every published breakpoint combined — because the breakpoints were never about you and your six entries are about nothing else.
