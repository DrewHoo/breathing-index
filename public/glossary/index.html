Glossary

What's in the air

One entry for every row the app can draw: what the number measures, what it does to breathing, the span it covers, and where it came from.

Fine particles

What it is Particles smaller than 2.5 µm (micrometers, millionths of a meter): smoke, exhaust, and particles that form in the air from gases. Small enough to reach the deepest parts of the lung.

How it affects breathing Seen in emergency-room studies: asthma visits rise about 4 % for every 10 µg/m³ (micrograms per cubic meter of air), and wildfire smoke hits harder per microgram than city particles do.

The window 24-hour average, because the health studies and the official breakpoints are daily.

Where the number comes from A monitor when one is within about 50 miles: an instrument run by your state environmental agency, published through the EPA’s AirNow. Otherwise a model: a computer estimate of the air from satellites, weather and emissions data on a 45-kilometer (28-mile) grid. A model is a prediction, not a measurement, and it can miss a plume a monitor would catch.

Ozone

What it is Ozone at ground level, made from traffic exhaust and heat in sunlight. Peaks mid-afternoon.

How it affects breathing Shown in lab studies: lung function drops and airways inflame after hours at 0.06 ppm (parts per million), below the US standard of 0.07, and exercise multiplies the dose because you breathe more of it. The effect lags by hours.

The window 8-hour average, the same span the US standard uses.

Where the number comes from A monitor when one is within about 50 miles: an instrument run by your state environmental agency, published through the EPA’s AirNow. Otherwise a model: a computer estimate of the air from satellites, weather and emissions data on a 45-kilometer (28-mile) grid. A model is a prediction, not a measurement, and it can miss a plume a monitor would catch. For ozone the model runs high in the eastern US in summer.

SO₂

What it is Sulfur dioxide (SO₂), a gas from coal, refineries, ships and volcanoes. Usually near zero in Connecticut.

How it affects breathing Shown in lab studies: people with asthma who are exercising tighten up within minutes at levels healthy lungs ignore. The best-proven sudden trigger there is, and the rarest here.

The window The hour itself. The row appears only when the gas is present.

Where the number comes from A monitor when one is within about 50 miles: an instrument run by your state environmental agency, published through the EPA’s AirNow. Otherwise a model: a computer estimate of the air from satellites, weather and emissions data on a 45-kilometer (28-mile) grid. A model is a prediction, not a measurement, and it can miss a plume a monitor would catch. Not every monitor measures SO₂; the line under the table says when none nearby does.

Coarse particles

What it is Particles smaller than 10 µm (micrometers). This includes the fine particles plus dust and road grit.

How it affects breathing Weak on its own for sudden asthma symptoms; it rises and falls with fine particles. Shown so you can see the coarse dust; the diary does not grade it.

The window 24-hour average.

Where the number comes from A monitor when one is within about 50 miles: an instrument run by your state environmental agency, published through the EPA’s AirNow. Otherwise a model: a computer estimate of the air from satellites, weather and emissions data on a 45-kilometer (28-mile) grid. A model is a prediction, not a measurement, and it can miss a plume a monitor would catch.

Smoke

What it is A smoke plume drawn over this place by an analyst at NOAA (the US weather agency) from satellite pictures, rated light, medium or heavy. Counted only when the particles at ground level are mostly fine ones, which is what smoke is made of.

How it affects breathing Seen in emergency-room studies: asthma visits rose 82 % in New York in one day of June 2023 smoke, and per microgram, smoke is two to three times as bad as ordinary fine particles.

The window The current hour. Satellites need daylight to see smoke, so at night the plume shown is the afternoon’s.

Where the number comes from NOAA’s Hazard Mapping System, plus the fine-particle reading above it.

Mold

What it is Outdoor fungal spores in the air, in spores/m³ (spores per cubic meter of air), counted under a microscope by the counting station you chose in Settings. Alternaria and Cladosporium are the two kinds that matter for asthma.

How it affects breathing Seen in emergency-room studies: every kind of fungal spore is linked to more asthma visits than any kind of pollen, and Alternaria is linked to near-fatal attacks. Spores are small enough to reach the lower airways.

The window The highest of the station’s last three counts, because the effect builds over a few days.

Where the number comes from A counting station: a hospital, health department or clinic that collects spores on a glass slide and counts them on weekday mornings. A count older than three days is shown as an estimate.

Dry-spore conditions

What it is A guess at dry-air spores from the weather alone: warm, dry, windy, no rain in two days, after a wet spell. Each condition met adds a point, out of five.

How it affects breathing The same spores as mold, by a stand-in: a mechanism, thin data. It can raise a suspicion and never confirm one.

The window Daily, in season only, always an estimate.

Where the number comes from No instrument looked at the air. Five weather conditions off the forecast, counted.

Tree pollen

What it is Tree pollen on a 0–5 scale, by kind of tree.

How it affects breathing Mostly a hay-fever story; the asthma evidence is thin, so these warn later than grass does.

The window The day’s value.

Where the number comes from Estimated by Google’s pollen model from land cover and weather, not counted with an instrument. A season calendar fills in where the model is silent, and says so.

Grass pollen

What it is Grass pollen on a 0–5 scale.

How it affects breathing Seen in emergency-room studies: the only pollen with a firm asthma signal, and it builds over three days.

The window The highest of the last three days, because the effect builds.

Where the number comes from Estimated by Google’s pollen model from land cover and weather, not counted with an instrument. A season calendar fills in where the model is silent, and says so.

Weed pollen

What it is Weed pollen on a 0–5 scale, by kind of weed. Ragweed is the big one.

How it affects breathing Mostly a hay-fever story; the asthma evidence is thin, so these warn later than grass does.

The window The day’s value.

Where the number comes from Estimated by Google’s pollen model from land cover and weather, not counted with an instrument. A season calendar fills in where the model is silent, and says so.

Dew point

What it is The temperature at which the water vapor in the air would condense into a dew drop. If it’s too high (above 18 °C / 64 °F), the air is hot and wet — muggy — and hot, humid air sets off a reflex that tightens airways. If it’s too low (below 11 °C / 52 °F), the air is dry enough to dry out the lining of the airways, which is what people call cold-air asthma. In between is comfortable.

How it affects breathing Two mechanisms, both shown in lab studies. On the dry side, what people call cold-air asthma is drying, not cold, and it needs hard breathing to start; breathing through the nose nearly cancels it. On the humid side, a separate reflex that an inhaler drug blocked in the lab.

The window The hour itself.

Where the number comes from The weather forecast’s hourly dew point. Always a model number, because no air-quality monitor reports it.

Sick

How it affects breathing A cold alone does little; a cold plus the pollen you react to does a lot. Logging it is what lets the diary see that.

Breathing Index is a logbook lens on public air data — not medical advice. Trust your symptoms and your asthma action plan over anything on this screen.
