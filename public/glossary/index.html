Glossary

What's in the air

One entry for every row the app can draw: what the number measures, what it does to breathing, the span it covers, and where it came from.

Fine particles

What it is Particles under 2.5 µm: smoke, exhaust, secondary aerosol. Small enough to reach the deep lung.

How it affects breathing Seen in emergency-visit studies: asthma visits rise about 4 % per 10 µg/m³, and wildfire smoke hits harder per microgram than city particulate.

The window 24-hour mean, because the health studies and the official breakpoints are daily.

Where the number comes from Monitor when one is near; the model otherwise.

What the words on the row mean The usual verdicts.

Ozone

What it is Ozone at ground level, made from traffic and heat in sunlight. Peaks mid-afternoon.

How it affects breathing Shown in controlled exposure: lung function drops and airways inflame at 0.06 ppm over hours, below the US standard, and exertion multiplies the dose. The effect lags by hours.

The window 8-hour mean, the standard's own form.

Where the number comes from Monitor or model; the model runs high in the eastern US in summer.

What the words on the row mean The usual verdicts.

SO₂

What it is Sulfur dioxide, from coal, refineries, ships and volcanoes. Usually near zero in Connecticut.

How it affects breathing Shown in controlled exposure: exercising asthmatics tighten within minutes at levels healthy lungs ignore. The best-proven acute trigger there is, and the rarest here.

The window The hour itself. The row appears only when it is present.

Where the number comes from Monitor where one reports it; the model otherwise.

What the words on the row mean The usual verdicts.

Coarse particles

What it is Particles under 10 µm, which includes PM2.5 plus dust and road grit.

How it affects breathing Weak on its own for acute asthma; it moves with PM2.5. Shown so you can see coarse particulate; the diary does not grade it.

The window 24-hour mean.

Where the number comes from Monitor when one is near; the model otherwise.

What the words on the row mean "not graded": shown, never a suspect.

Smoke

What it is NOAA's satellite smoke analysis, light to heavy, counted only when the particulate under it is fine-mode.

How it affects breathing Seen in emergency-visit studies: asthma visits rose 82 % in New York in a day of June 2023 smoke; per microgram, smoke is two to three times ordinary PM2.5.

The window The current hour; satellites need daylight, so at night the plume is the afternoon's.

Where the number comes from An analyst's plume drawn over this place, from NOAA's Hazard Mapping System.

What the words on the row mean The usual verdicts.

Mold

What it is Outdoor fungal spores per cubic metre, counted on a slide by the station you chose. Alternaria and Cladosporium are the two that matter.

How it affects breathing Seen in emergency-visit studies: every fungal group larger than any pollen group; Alternaria is linked to near-fatal asthma. Spores are small enough to reach the lower airway.

The window Highest of the station's last three counts.

Where the number comes from Stations count on weekday mornings; a count older than three days is an estimate.

What the words on the row mean "estimate" when the count is old.

Dry-spore conditions

What it is A weather guess at dry-air spores: warm, dry, windy, no rain in two days, after a wet spell.

How it affects breathing The same spores as mold, by proxy. It can suspect and never confirm.

The window Daily, in season, always an estimate.

Where the number comes from No instrument looked at the air: five weather conditions off the forecast, counted.

What the words on the row mean "estimate from weather".

Tree pollen

What it is Tree pollen on a 0–5 index, by plant.

How it affects breathing Mostly a hay-fever story; the asthma evidence is thin, so these warn later than grass.

The window The day's index.

Where the number comes from Modelled, not counted; a calendar estimate where the model is silent.

What the words on the row mean "calendar estimate".

Grass pollen

What it is Grass pollen on a 0–5 index, by plant.

How it affects breathing Seen in emergency-visit studies: the only pollen with a firm asthma signal, and it builds over three days.

The window Highest of the last three days.

Where the number comes from Modelled, not counted; a calendar estimate where the model is silent.

What the words on the row mean "calendar estimate".

Weed pollen

What it is Weed pollen on a 0–5 index, by plant.

How it affects breathing Mostly a hay-fever story; the asthma evidence is thin, so these warn later than grass.

The window The day's index.

Where the number comes from Modelled, not counted; a calendar estimate where the model is silent.

What the words on the row mean "calendar estimate".

Dew point

What it is One measurement with two edges. Dew point below 11 °C / 52 °F: air dry enough to dry the airway lining. Dew point above 18 °C / 64 °F: hot, wet air.

How it affects breathing Two mechanisms, one row. On the dry side, shown in controlled exposure: what people call cold-air asthma is drying, not cold, and it needs hard breathing to start. Nose-breathing nearly cancels it. On the humid side, also shown in controlled exposure: a separate reflex from dry air, blocked by an inhaler drug in the lab.

The window The hour itself.

Where the number comes from The weather forecast's hourly dew point — modelled everywhere, since no air-quality monitor reports it.

What the words on the row mean "comfortable" between 11 and 18 °C.

Sick

What it is You said you were sick.

How it affects breathing A cold alone does little; a cold plus the pollen you react to does a lot. Logging it is what lets the diary see that.

The window The day you tapped it.

Where the number comes from You, not a feed: the one variable here nobody measures.

What the words on the row mean No words on the air table — being sick is not air. It earns a line in the diary’s evidence panel instead.

Breathing Index is a logbook lens on public air data — not medical advice. Trust your symptoms and your asthma action plan over anything on this screen.
